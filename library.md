# The Dar Kinnod Library

## Dar Kinnod - The Song of Stone

### The Creation

Hear now the words of Kulena, she who keeps the river of time aflow. She speaks the breath of the world onto the scrolls. → Jas zipo re kin-Kulena. Katoac solazo. Ruac lifex ti atazu gu cizu.
She writes of the Gods and of their quarrels, there in their house of stone and gold. → Falac ti lorevu g ucmorfu, nev ga uceno ti nod g lox.
She writes of the beginning, before the age of Man, before the age of the sun and the moon. → Falac la vixa, ezop rukohej, ezop hej ti iwa g umi.
In those days the Gods are weary of one another, and swift to anger. → Kosa k hej, lorevu tul mogeuc, tul nasovetanuc kenzewo.
Many a day they war over things both great and small, and their wounds they leave to fester. → Mortuc hex la ojaopu g ojafu, o ucmavu tu xonu.
But Kulena, wisest of the Gods, sees that this cannot endure, and she devises a means of salvation. → Tirel Kulena, rutenop ti lorevu, fideac k oja iredono fem e ral, o rakac sozrom.
"Let us turn our eyes from one another," she says to them, "and make a new world, and a people to dwell within it, whose lives we may guide and shape according to our will." → Menac nak uc, mortug femop, o raktug dalatazu, g ruko gol id nev, osa idexu iredotug kato g levo ken tuglunirex.
And all the Gods give their assent, and the great work begins. → O lorevu evohenoc, o dabop vixano.
Etovo, smith of the Gods, forges moulds to hold the bodies of Men. → Etovo, lokrom ti lorevu, lokac levoromu ipe tortan rukoidbaru.
Then Kulena the all-wise plucks from her head a single strand of hair. → Kulena rutenop atoac zipo tuop koje nik zat.
It shimmers as she draws it forth, and she breaks it into seven parts. → Koje uzono kosa pirolam, o berac sa tufu.
And as she lays them down, the pieces turn into little stars, and she lightly breathes to them a will and a knowing. → O kosa vegoam, tufu levonu kan uzofu, o lifac kencawi nak uc lunirex g fiderom.
She turns to Etovo and proclaims: "Behold my making: the souls of Men. To thee I leave the shaping of their vessels." → Exoac kan Etovo, o menopac, Hitan zipo tagrakex, rukoidromu. Ilitag nak wet levoam ti ucidbaru.
And at her words, a flame awakens in Etovo's lungs. → O kosa acre, vulfo kasno ga lifrom kin Etovo.
And so Etovo, consumed by the will to create, lays his ingots in the crucible and sets it upon the fire. → O Etovo, ka lunirop ipe rak, vegoac achosu ga vembar, o no vegoac gu vul.
As the metal melts, he fills it with the essence of all the Gods. → Kosa vemam, no vecoac ka nocenex.
Kulena pours her wisdom. → Kulena sajac acruten.
Javi pours his strength. → Javi sajac acvorex.
Kemac pours her love. → Kemac sajac acgerex.
And Etovo pours his skill. → O Etovo sajac acdor.
When all runs molten, he casts it into the rough moulds of Men, and draws them forth, and begins to shape them in his image. → Kosa nocvemam, no sajac kan k jeholevoromu, o nu pirolac, o vixac nu levo kan ackuma.
He hammers the fire-metal, and their shapes are rough no more. → Zekac vulsagi, o nukumu jehonu femop.
Then he looks upon their shapes, and they please him. And he takes up the souls that Kulena has made, and lays them within his creations, like jewels in a crown. → Hiac zipo nukumu, o nu gerfoac. O atoac rukoidromu kin Kulena, o uc vegoac ga acrakexu, ken uzonodu ga zatlox.
And for a moment nothing moves; then they tremble, and stir, and rise. → Kosa solfo, femher gilno. Aruc zipo, o giluc, o kutuc.
And soon they ask why they were made, and they are quick of hand and mind. → O dajecuc, uc miraktul hid, o zewouc ka atu g fideromu.
They speak of love, and they speak of sorrow. → Ruc la gerex, o ruc la kora.
And his heart goes out to them, his creation, and he swears to them a home of their own. → O demac kora ipe uc, acrakex, o ilizorac nak uc eno kin uc.
To keep them warm, Etovo draws from his forge the hottest of its embers and lays it in the palm of his hand. → Ipe uc turtan, Etovo atoac nik aclokbar turopvulfo, o no vegoac ga fih.
He closes his fingers upon it and presses it with all his might, and within his grasp it burns red, then yellow, then white. → Leac nesu gu no, o no torac ka vorop, o ga acfih vireno, ke vimano, ke vileno.

 And when he opens his hand, the ember holds its glow, and he is content.

And to light their way, Etovo works the bellows, and plucks the sparks that leap from his forge, and lays them upon his finest silk.

Then he takes from his hoard a cold ingot and turns it in his hands, and in it he sees the world that he will shape for Man. So he lays it upon his anvil, and with his hammer he strikes it with great blows, until it breaks in two. One half he presses between his palms and rolls into a sphere. And when it is round, he dips it into his quenching trough, so that the waters gather in its lowest places. And the other half he crushes roughly in his palm. And he sets the ember and both halves upon the silk among the sparks, and folds the silk closed around them.

Thus does Etovo make the world. And he turns to the Gods and proclaims: "Behold, my brothers and sisters, the work of my hands!" And all who see it are struck with awe.

Then Javi steps forth and takes hold of the silk, and with a mighty sweep of his arm he sets in motion the sun and the stars, the world and the moon, so that day gives way to night, and night to day.

Thus it is written by Kulena, mother of scribes and keeper of all that is recorded. Her words endure, and we recite them still, lest we forget who we are.

### Before Time

Thus it begins: the age of sun and stars, of earth and moon. In those realms dwell Men, who know neither fear nor hunger nor sleep. They raise no houses and walk no roads, and no word passes between them, for they have no need of words. The essence of the Gods runs strong in them, and their days are given to music, to the carving of stone, and to love. They feast only for delight. At first the Gods rejoice, and in their gladness they forget their quarrels. They keep watch over the realm of Men and take pleasure in their works. Yet time moves on, and Men do not change. Content with their lot, they spend each day as they spent the last.

But in time the Gods grow weary of Men. The ebb and flow of the endless days grows dull to them. They turn their eyes once more upon one another, and the old quarrels wake again. Then Kulena's sorrow swells ever greater, and the weight of failure lies heavy on her shoulders. Javi the almighty sees his beloved in her grief. He perceives the flaw in her design, and he devises a design of his own. While the eyes of his brethren are turned elsewhere, he bends his gaze upon the realm of Men. He raises a finger of his right hand, and with one swift stroke of his dagger he draws forth a single drop of his godly blood. Into it he whispers of struggle and of pain, of hunger and of death. Then he lets the drop fall into the ocean, and quickly it spreads through the seas, the rivers and the lakes. And when the rains come, they pour down upon the realm of Men. The blood-tainted water washes over their bodies and seeps in through their skin, and the taint takes hold of them.

Soon a change comes upon them. Their minds grow heavy with weariness, their bellies ache if they do not eat, and their throats parch if they do not drink. In confusion and torment they cry out to the Gods, but no answer comes. Then Men know that they are cursed. To quench their thirst, they drink from the rivers. To still their hunger, they sow the fields. To shield themselves from heat and cold, they raise houses. But still it is not enough. Their flesh withers with the years, and they pass from the world. Great sorrow falls upon them, and they sink into despair. Yet something within them bids them go on, for the essence of the Gods runs deep in their veins. And so they endure.

Now the Gods look on in wonder. The struggles of Men become their theatre. They pity these small creatures and meddle in their affairs, giving and taking away. Here they make the crops bear richly; there they dry up a spring. They raise high mountains and fair plains that stir the minds of Men, yet they also loose cruel beasts to roam the earth and hunt the children of Men. Into the hearts of some they breathe love; into the ears of others they whisper jealousy.

To live, Men must hunt the beasts and till the land. And as their numbers swell, the land can no longer sustain them. Some leave with broken hearts to seek new plains far away. Others turn to strife and theft.

### The Song of Stone

## Prologue

On the morning of February 11th, 1912, you receive this letter, addressed personally to you, at your office.

```
My dear Sitwell,

It is with the greatest pleasure, and no small excitement, that I write to you today from Tiflis, at the very edge of the European continent.

Last year, our archaeological endeavours led Gregory Chamfer and me into the remotest reaches of the Caucasus, where we made the most remarkable discoveries: the remains of an ancient civilisation, long lost to time, which has puzzled us ever since. Its people settled in every crevice, nook and cave, and yet scarcely a trace of them remains.

Among the few artefacts they left behind is a book, beautifully preserved and quite unlike anything I have ever seen. Its pages are written in a strange script whose ancestry we have been wholly unable to trace. Happily, the script is not altogether dead. My guide, Irakli, found a few villagers who still make use of it; and though they could recognise its letters, they could make nothing of the language itself. With their help, we drew up a guide to the alphabet, by means of which we transliterated the script into Latin letters.

What we found was most extraordinary. The language bears not the slightest resemblance to any of the Kartvelian tongues, nor to the Indo-European or Semitic families, nor indeed to any other we could find. Even with our guide in hand, we could not make out a single word, nor so much as guess at its meaning. And so I turn to you, old friend. If any linguist in England can solve this riddle, it is you.

By the same post I have dispatched a bundle containing the book and the other artefacts found with it, together with our guide to this most curious alphabet. I trust you will find them of use in deciphering this unknown tongue. 

Godspeed, old friend.

Yours ever,

Aldermoor

P.S. Should you ever need me, dispatch a telegraph to the Tiflis office. A man of mine there will carry any word up to me in the mountains.
```

## Chapter 1

After traveling to the Caucasus, Aldermoore uses the map to locate the monument to Etovo. Inside a cave, a groin-vaulted chamber. Directly in front of the entrance, a pedestal with the feet of a broken statue. Its arm, holding a hammer, lays on the ground. The rest of the statue is missing. At its base, a stone tablet with inscriptions. Around the room, in the four corners of supporting walls, large stone bowls supported by a metal structure. The end of each barrel vault is blocked, except the first. Clockwise: entrance, depiction of a hammer, a forge, depiction of the bellows.

Ffrom the entrance, in front of the statue, a lectern with a singular, well preserved book.

### Riddles

On the back wall of one of the barrel vaults, a mural depticting Etovo in 4 different poses. Each pose is part of the progression of a story. In each pose, two holes. Below the mural, on the floor, twelve circular stones that fit into the slots with a pictogram. Below each pose, a poem.

Storyline and answers:
```
1. From the forge, Etovo takes an ember with his tongs and crushes it into the sun (tongs, sun).
2. Etovo swells the bellows and plucks sparks from the forge, placing them on silk to create the stars (bellows, stars)
3. With his hammer, Etovo breaks a chunk on ingot and creates the Earth and the Moon (earth, moon)
4. Etovo melts his ingots and pours them into the moulds of Men (ingot, fire)
```
